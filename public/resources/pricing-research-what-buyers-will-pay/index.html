Pricing research: how to find what buyers will pay.

You don't have to guess a price. Buyers already pay for something that solves the same problem, and much of that information is public. Start there, build a price band, then test it.

10 to 20 real price points

The method in five steps

  • Collect 10 to 20 public price points for the closest alternatives.
  • Convert them to one unit so they can be compared.
  • Build a band: lowest, highest, median and the middle half.
  • Study how the alternatives are packaged.
  • Test your price with real buyers before you commit.

1. Collect real price points

List what buyers pay today for the nearest alternatives. Include direct competitors, substitutes and the "do it by hand" option. Good sources are pricing pages, marketplace listings, public proposals and prices quoted in forums and reviews. Record the source and the date for every number.

2. Convert to one unit

Prices come per seat, per project, per month or per year. Pick the unit your buyer thinks in and convert everything to it. Without this step the comparison is meaningless.

3. Build the price band

FigureWhat it tells you
Lowest and highestThe edges of what the market tolerates
MedianThe price a typical buyer already accepts
Middle halfThe range where most alternatives sit. Pricing far outside it needs a clear reason.
GapsPrice levels nobody serves. Some are openings. Some are empty for a reason.

4. Study the packaging

Look at what each tier includes, what is held back for higher tiers and what is charged as an extra. Packaging shows what the seller believes buyers value most. It is often easier to compete on packaging than on the number.

5. Count the switching cost

A buyer compares your price with the cost of leaving what they have: time, training, data migration and risk. If switching is painful, a lower price alone won't move them. Your offer has to reduce that pain.

6. Test with buyers

  • Conversations. Five to ten buyer conversations will show where the price feels wrong and why.
  • A price-sensitivity survey. The Van Westendorp method asks four questions about when a price feels too cheap, a bargain, getting expensive and too expensive.
  • A real offer. The strongest test is asking someone to pay or sign. Interest costs nothing. Commitment is evidence.

What each kind of evidence is worth

EvidenceWhat it showsIts limit
Public price pointsWhat buyers already acceptList prices can differ from what is actually paid
Buyer conversationsThe reasons behind price resistancePeople are kinder in conversation than at checkout
SurveysA range across many buyersStated intent isn't the same as buying
Paid pilots or pre-ordersReal willingness to paySmall samples, and early adopters aren't typical

Questions people ask

Should a new product be priced below competitors?

Not automatically. A price far below the band can signal lower quality, and it leaves little room to grow. Price inside or near the band unless you have a clear reason and a model that supports it.

How many price points do I need?

Ten to twenty is enough for a first band. Fewer than ten and a single outlier can distort the picture.

How do I price something with no direct competitor?

Price against what buyers do today instead: the manual work, the internal hire, or the nearest substitute. That cost is your comparison.
