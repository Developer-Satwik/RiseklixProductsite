Competitor analysis: how to see how rivals actually execute.

Most competitor analyses are a table of features. That tells you what a rival sells. It doesn't tell you how they win. These six signals show what a competitor is really doing, using information that is already public.

Six signals to read

  • Positioning: who they say they are for.
  • Pricing: what they charge and how they package it.
  • Channels: where they find customers.
  • Hiring: where they are investing next.
  • Reviews: what customers praise and complain about.
  • AI answers: how often assistants recommend them, and why.

The teardown template

How to read each signal

Positioning

Write down each competitor's headline promise in one line. If three of them say the same thing, that position is taken. Look for the buyer nobody is speaking to.

Pricing

Record the public price points and the unit they charge by: per seat, per project, per month. How a rival packages is often more revealing than the number. See how to find what buyers will pay.

Channels

Note where each competitor shows up repeatedly. A rival that sponsors every industry event has made that route expensive. A channel nobody uses may be an opening, or may be empty for a reason.

Hiring

Open roles are a public roadmap. Six sales roles in one country means a push into that market. A run of engineering hires in one area means a product bet.

Reviews

Read the three-star reviews first. They name what works and what doesn't in the same breath. Repeated complaints are your clearest opening.

AI answers

Ask the questions a buyer would ask and record who gets named. In our own research, the firm that kept appearing was often not the best known. It was the best documented for that exact situation.

Turning five teardowns into a decision

  1. Put the five teardowns side by side, one row per signal.
  2. Mark where everyone does the same thing. That is the cost of entry.
  3. Mark where nobody is strong. Those are candidate positions.
  4. Check each candidate against demand: is there evidence buyers want it?
  5. Pick one position you can defend, and the first test that would prove it.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing features and ignoring how the competitor sells.
  • Trusting what a competitor claims over what its customers say.
  • Studying only the market leader, and missing the specialist who wins a niche.
  • Doing it once. Markets move, so date every observation.

Questions people ask

How many competitors should I analyse?

Five is a good start: the leader, two direct rivals, one specialist and one substitute that buyers use instead. Add more by segment once you know which position you are testing.

Is it legal to research competitors this way?

This method uses information the competitor has made public: websites, pricing pages, job adverts, reviews and public answers from AI tools. It doesn't involve private or confidential material.

How often should a competitor analysis be updated?

Review it when you plan a launch or a pricing change, and at least a few times a year. Date every observation so you can see what moved.
